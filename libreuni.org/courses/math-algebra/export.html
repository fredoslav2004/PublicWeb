Group Theory Fundamentals

Group Theory Fundamentals

Group theory is the mathematical study of symmetry. It provides a formal framework for analyzing operations that preserve the structure of an object.

Formal Definition of a Group

A group (G,⋅)(G, \cdot) is a set GG together with a binary operation ⋅\cdot that satisfies:

  1. Closure: a,b∈G  ⟹  a⋅b∈Ga, b \in G \implies a \cdot b \in G.
  2. Associativity: (a⋅b)⋅c=a⋅(b⋅c)(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c).
  3. Identity Element: ∃e∈G:e⋅a=a\exists e \in G: e \cdot a = a.
  4. Inverse Element: ∀a∈G,∃b∈G:a⋅b=e\forall a \in G, \exists b \in G: a \cdot b = e.

If a⋅b=b⋅aa \cdot b = b \cdot a, the group is Abelian.

The order of a group ∣G∣|G| is its size. The order of an element gg is the smallest nn such that gn=eg^n = e. If a single element generates the whole group, it is Cyclic.

Advanced Group Theory

Advanced Group Theory

Advanced group theory explores the internal structure of groups through cosets, normality, and factor groups.

Lagrange’s Theorem

Lagrange’s Theorem states that for any finite group GG, the order of every subgroup HH of GG divides the order of GG. ∣G∣=[G:H]⋅∣H∣|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H| where [G:H][G : H] is the number of distinct cosets of HH in GG.

Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups

A subgroup N⊴GN \trianglelefteq G is normal if gN=NggN = Ng for all g∈Gg \in G. Normal subgroups allow the construction of the quotient group G/NG/N, where elements are cosets and the operation is (aN)(bN)=(ab)N(aN)(bN) = (ab)N.

The First Isomorphism Theorem

Let ϕ:G→H\phi: G \to H be a group homomorphism. Then:

  1. ker⁡(ϕ)\ker(\phi) is a normal subgroup of GG.
  2. im(ϕ)\text{im}(\phi) is a subgroup of HH.
  3. G/ker⁡(ϕ)≅im(ϕ)G/\ker(\phi) \cong \text{im}(\phi).

Group Actions and Orbit-Stabilizer

A group GG acts on a set XX if there is a map G×X→XG \times X \to X satisfying identity and compatibility axioms. The Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem relates the size of the orbit G⋅xG \cdot x and the stabilizer GxG_x: ∣G∣=∣G⋅x∣⋅∣Gx∣|G| = |G \cdot x| \cdot |G_x|

Rings, Fields, and Integral Domains

Rings, Fields, and Integral Domains

While group theory deals with a single binary operation, rings and fields formalize structures with two operations: Addition and Multiplication.

The Definition of a Ring

A ring (R,+,⋅)(R, +, \cdot) satisfies:

  1. (R,+)(R, +) is an Abelian Group.
  2. Multiplication is Associative.
  3. Distributive Laws: a(b+c)=ab+aca(b+c) = ab+ac.

Ideals and Quotient Rings

An Ideal II of a ring RR is a subring such that for every r∈Rr \in R and i∈Ii \in I, the products riri and irir are in II.

  • Formally: (R,+)(R, +) is a subgroup, and ∀r∈R,∀i∈I:r⋅i∈I and i⋅r∈I\forall r \in R, \forall i \in I: r \cdot i \in I \text{ and } i \cdot r \in I.

If II is an ideal, the Quotient Ring R/IR/I is the set of cosets {r+I:r∈R}\{r + I : r \in R\} under the operations:

  • (a+I)+(b+I)=(a+b)+I(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I
  • (a+I)⋅(b+I)=(ab)+I(a + I) \cdot (b + I) = (ab) + I

Ring Homomorphisms

A mapping f:R→Sf: R \to S between two rings is a homomorphism if it preserves both operations:

  1. f(a+b)=f(a)+f(b)f(a + b) = f(a) + f(b)
  2. f(ab)=f(a)f(b)f(ab) = f(a)f(b)
  3. f(1R)=1Sf(1_R) = 1_S (for rings with identity)

The Kernel of a homomorphism ker⁡(f)={r∈R:f(r)=0S}\ker(f) = \{r \in R : f(r) = 0_S\} is always an ideal of RR.

Field Theory and Extensions

Field Theory and Extensions

Fields are algebraic structures where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (by non-zero elements) are well-defined.

Characteristic of a Field

The characteristic of a field FF, denoted char(F)\text{char}(F), is the smallest p>0p > 0 such that p⋅1=0p \cdot 1 = 0. If no such pp exists, the characteristic is zero.

Field Extensions

If F⊆KF \subseteq K, we say KK is a field extension of FF. KK is then a vector space over FF. The dimension [K:F][K:F] is the degree of the extension.

Algebraic and Transcendental Elements

An element α∈K\alpha \in K is algebraic over FF if it is a root of some non-zero polynomial p(x)∈F[x]p(x) \in F[x]. Otherwise, α\alpha is transcendental.

Galois Theory

Galois Theory

Galois theory connects field theory and group theory, providing a way to study field extensions by examining their automorphism groups.

Galois Groups

The Galois group of a field extension K/FK/F, denoted Gal(K/F)\text{Gal}(K/F), is the group of automorphisms of KK that fix FF point-wise. σ∈Gal(K/F)  ⟺  σ(a)=a for all a∈F\sigma \in \text{Gal}(K/F) \iff \sigma(a) = a \text{ for all } a \in F

Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

For a finite Galois extension K/FK/F, there is a one-to-one correspondence between:

  1. Subfields EE such that F⊆E⊆KF \subseteq E \subseteq K.
  2. Subgroups HH of Gal(K/F)\text{Gal}(K/F).

The mapping is defined by E↦Gal(K/E)E \mapsto \text{Gal}(K/E) and H↦KHH \mapsto K^H (the fixed field of HH).

Solvability by Radicals

A polynomial equation is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group. This explains why there is no general formula for the roots of a quintic (fifth-degree) polynomial, as the symmetric group S5S_5 is not solvable.
