Foundations of Probability

Foundations of Probability

Probability is the mathematical language of uncertainty. It allows us to quantify the likelihood of events in a rigorous way.

1. The Rules of the Game: Kolmogorov Axioms

In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov formalized probability using three axioms for a Sample Space (Ω\Omega):

  1. Non-negativity: P(A)≥0P(A) \ge 0.
  2. Normalization: P(Ω)=1P(\Omega) = 1.
  3. Additivity: If A∩B=∅A \cap B = \emptyset, then P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B).

2. The Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

The LLN states that as the number of trials increases, the actual ratio of outcomes will converge to the theoretical probability. This is why casinos always win in the long run, even if a player wins a single hand.

3. Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

How does our knowledge change when we get new information? The Conditional Probability of AA given BB is: P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B),for P(B)>0P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{for } P(B) > 0

Law of Total Probability

If {Bi}\{B_i\} is a partition of the sample space Ω\Omega, then for any event AA: P(A)=∑iP(A∣Bi)P(Bi)P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i)P(B_i)

Bayes’ Theorem

Bayes’ Theorem relates the “likelihood” P(B∣A)P(B|A) and the “prior” P(A)P(A) to the “posterior” P(A∣B)P(A|B): P(A∣B)=P(B∣A)P(A)P(B)P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}

This is the mathematical foundation for “updating” beliefs based on evidence.

4. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The CLT is the most important theorem in statistics. It states that the distribution of the sum (or average) of many independent random variables tends toward a Normal Distribution, regardless of the original shape.

Statistical Inference

Statistical Inference

Probability tells us how to predict data if we know the rules of the game. Statistical Inference is the reverse: we have the data, and we want to figure out the rules. If you poll 1000 people and 520 say they will vote for Candidate A, can you reasonably conclude that Candidate A is winning the whole election?

Point Estimation

A point estimate is a single “best guess” for a population parameter (like the mean μ\mu or variance σ2\sigma^2). The most widely used method for finding these estimates is Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). The idea is simple: choose the parameter values that make the observed data “most likely” to have happened.

Scenario: Coin Flipping

If you flip a coin 10 times and get 7 heads, what is the probability pp of getting a head?

  • The likelihood function L(p)=p7(1−p)3L(p) = p^7(1-p)^3.
  • To find the maximum, we take the derivative and set it to zero.
  • The result is p^=0.7\hat{p} = 0.7. Our “best guess” is that the coin is biased.

Confidence Intervals

A point estimate is never perfect. A Confidence Interval (CI) provides a range of values that is likely to contain the true parameter. A “95% Confidence Interval” means that if we repeated the experiment 100 times, 95 of our calculated intervals would contain the true value.

The formula for a CI of a mean is typically: xˉ±z∗σn\bar{x} \pm z^* \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} where σn\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} is the Standard Error, which decreases as our sample size nn increases.

Bias and Variance

Two things can go wrong with an estimator:

  1. Bias: On average, is the guess too high or too low? (Accuracy)
  2. Variance: How much does the guess change if we get a new sample? (Precision)

In Machine Learning, this is the Bias-Variance Tradeoff. Simple models (like a straight line) have high bias but low variance. Complex models (like deep neural networks) have low bias but high variance, leading to “overfitting.”

Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing is the backbone of the scientific method. It is a formal process for deciding whether a claim about a population is likely to be true, given the evidence in a sample. In business, this is called A/B Testing. In medicine, it is a Clinical Trial.

The Null and Alternative Hypotheses

We start with two competing claims:

  1. Null Hypothesis (H0H_0): The “status quo.” There is no effect, no difference, or nothing interesting happening.
  2. Alternative Hypothesis (H1H_1 or HaH_a): The claim we are trying to prove. There is a difference or an effect.

Crucial Point: In statistics, we never “prove” H1H_1 is true. We only determine if we have enough evidence to reject H0H_0.

P-Values: The Probability of Being Wrong

The p-value is the probability of seeing results as extreme as yours if the null hypothesis were actually true.

  • A small p-value (usually < 0.05) means the data is very unlikely under H0H_0. We reject the null.
  • A large p-value means the data is perfectly normal under H0H_0. We fail to reject the null.

Type I and Type II Errors

  • Type I (False Positive): Rejecting H0H_0 when it was actually true. (Convicting an innocent person).
  • Type II (False Negative): Failing to reject H0H_0 when it was actually false. (Letting a guilty person go).

The T-Test: Comparing Means

The T-test is used to determine if the means of two groups are significantly different from each other. For example, does a new website design lead to more sales than the old one?

Effect Size vs. Statistical Significance

A result can be “statistically significant” even if the difference is tiny, provided the sample size is large enough. Effect Size (like Cohen’s d) measures the magnitude of the difference. In the real world, we want results that are both statistically significant AND practically meaningful.

Bayesian Statistics

Bayesian Statistics

Frequentist statistics (p-values, confidence intervals) treats probability as the long-run frequency of events. Bayesian statistics treats probability as a degree of belief. When new data arrives, we don’t start from scratch; we update our existing beliefs.

Bayes’ Theorem: The Updating Rule

The core of Bayesian stats is Bayes’ Theorem:

P(H∣D)=P(D∣H)⋅P(H)P(D)P(H|D) = \frac{P(D|H) \cdot P(H)}{P(D)}

Where:

  • P(H∣D)P(H|D) (Posterior): Our belief in the hypothesis HH after seeing data DD.
  • P(D∣H)P(D|H) (Likelihood): How likely is the data DD if HH were true?
  • P(H)P(H) (Prior): Our belief in HH before seeing the data.
  • P(D)P(D) (Evidence): The total probability of seeing the data.

The Power of the Prior

Imagine you test positive for a very rare disease. A frequentist might look at the test’s 99% accuracy and conclude you are 99% likely to be sick. A Bayesian looks at the Prior: if only 1 in 10,000 people have the disease, even a positive test result doesn’t make it likely you’re sick—it just makes it more likely than before.

Bayesian Updating in Action

Let’s simulate “learning” the fairness of a coin. We start with a “Uniform Prior” (we think any fairness level from 0 to 1 is equally likely). As we flip the coin and see “Heads,” our belief (the Posterior) shifts.

Why Use Bayesian Stats?

  1. Incorporates Expert Knowledge: Priors allow you to start with what you already know.
  2. Intuitive Results: You can say “There is a 95% probability the parameter is in this range,” which frequentists are technically not allowed to say.
  3. Works with Small Data: When you have very little data, your Prior keeps your estimates from being wild.

Markov Chains

Markov Chains

A Markov Chain is a stochastic model describing a sequence of possible events in which the probability of each event depends only on the state attained in the previous event. This is known as the Markov Property: “The future is independent of the past, given the present.”

States and Transitions

A Markov Chain consists of:

  1. States (SS): The possible conditions the system can be in (e.g., Sunny, Rainy).
  2. Transition Matrix (PP): A matrix where the entry PijP_{ij} represents the probability of moving from state ii to state jj.

The sum of each row in a transition matrix must always be 1, because the system must go somewhere.

The Transition Matrix

Imagine a simple weather model:

  • If it is Sunny today, there is a 90% chance it will be Sunny tomorrow and 10% Rainy.
  • If it is Rainy today, there is a 50% chance it will be Sunny tomorrow and 50% Rainy.

The transition matrix PP looks like this:

P=(0.90.10.50.5)P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}

Predicting the Future

To find the state distribution after nn steps, we simply multiply our initial state vector v0v_0 by the matrix PP raised to the power of nn:

vn=v0⋅Pnv_n = v_0 \cdot P^n

Stationary Distribution

In many Markov Chains, after many steps, the state distribution settles into a fixed pattern called the Stationary Distribution (π\pi). This is the distribution that doesn’t change when multiplied by the transition matrix:

πP=π\pi P = \pi

In our weather example, the stationary distribution is [0.833, 0.167]—meaning in the long run, it will be sunny about 83% of the time, regardless of how it started.

Stochastic Processes

Stochastic Processes

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables representing the evolution of some system over time. Unlike a deterministic system (like f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2), a stochastic system’s future can only be described using probabilities.

The Random Walk

The simplest stochastic process is the Random Walk. Imagine a person on a line who flips a coin:

  • Heads: Move +1 step.
  • Tails: Move -1 step.

Over time, where will they be? On average, they will be at 0, but the distance from 0 (the standard deviation) grows with the square root of time (t\sqrt{t}).

Brownian Motion (Wiener Process)

In calculus, we assume functions are smooth. In stochastic calculus, we use Brownian Motion, which is continuous but “infinitely wiggly.” It is the limit of a random walk as the steps become infinitely small and frequent.

Key properties of Brownian Motion WtW_t:

  1. W0=0W_0 = 0.
  2. It has independent increments: movement between [1,2][1, 2] doesn’t depend on [0,1][0, 1].
  3. Wt−Ws∼N(0,t−s)W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t-s).

Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)

GBM is the famous model used in finance (Black-Scholes) to model stock prices. It assumes that the log of the price follows a random walk with a “drift” (average growth).

dSt=μStdt+σStdWtdS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t

Where:

  • μ\mu: The drift (expected return).
  • σ\sigma: The volatility (risk).
